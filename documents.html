Historical Overview

Tripura in Ancient Indian Traditions and Historical Records

1. Tripura in the Mahabharata

The Mahabharata, one of the major Sanskrit epics of ancient India, contains a passage in the Sabha Parva describing Sahadeva's campaign and a ruler associated with Tripura. This reference is part of the epic tradition connected with the region. However, the identity of the epic's Tripura and the historical date of the events cannot be established from this passage alone.

Reference: Mahabharata, Sabha Parva, Book 2, Chapter 28, Verse 38 (commonly used numbering). Read the verse.

2. The Prayaga Prashasti of Samudragupta

The fourth-century CE Prayaga Prashasti, inscribed on the pillar at present-day Prayagraj, records frontier states including Samatata, Davaka, Kamarupa and Nepala, as well as a place-name commonly transcribed as Kartripura. Its identification remains debated, and it should not automatically be equated with Tripura. This inscription belongs to the Gupta emperor Samudragupta, not to Emperor Ashoka.

Further reading: Siddham inscription record.

3. Rajmala: The Royal Chronicle of Tripura

Rajmala, a royal chronicle composed in Bengali verse by scholars associated with Tripura's royal court, preserves dynastic traditions, genealogies and accounts of rulers. It is an important source for understanding Tripura's historical memory, although early genealogies and legendary narratives should be compared with inscriptions and other historical evidence.

Reference: James Long, Analysis of the Raj-mala, or Chronicles of Tripura (1850). Read the digitised work.

4. Tripura and the Wider History of Northeast India

Tripura's history forms part of the wider history of Northeast India, shaped by indigenous communities, political formations and cultural exchange. Accounts attributed to the Chinese Buddhist traveller Xuanzang should be checked against a reliable edition of his travel record before being quoted as direct evidence about Tripura or the peoples of the Brahmaputra valley.

5. Historical Assessment

Epic literature, royal chronicles, inscriptions and archaeological evidence offer different perspectives on Tripura's past. These sources must be assessed according to their dates, purposes and limitations. Respect for traditional accounts and careful evaluation of historical evidence together provide a more reliable understanding of Tripura's heritage.

References and Further Reading

Historical Geography

Boundaries of the State of Tripura

1. Historical Extent of the Tripura Kingdom

The ancient Kingdom of Tripura is traditionally described as having extended across a vast geographical area in eastern India and adjoining regions. Historical accounts describe its reach towards Burma (present-day Myanmar) in the east, the Bay of Bengal in the south, the Naga Hills in the north and North Bengal in the west. These descriptions reflect the kingdom's remembered historical extent, while its precise boundaries varied across different periods.

During the reign of Maharaja Bir Bikram Kishore Manikya Debbarma, the last reigning Maharaja before Tripura's accession to India, two distinct entities associated with the royal house were Hill Tipperah, the princely state, and Tipperah District, administered under British rule. Tipperah District and portions of the Chittagong Hill Tracts were associated with the royal estate known as Chakla Roshnabad, reported in the historical account consulted for this article to cover approximately 550 square miles. The estate and the princely state had different administrative statuses.

Tripura's territorial position changed over the centuries through political conflicts, the expansion of Mughal power in the Bengal region, and subsequent developments under British rule.

2. Tripura's Territorial Status under British Rule

During British rule, questions concerning Tripura's territorial integrity and political status arose on several occasions. According to the historical account consulted for this article, in 1861 the Commissioner of the Chittagong Hill Tracts sought to annex Tripura by force. The Governor-General of British India reportedly instructed him to refrain from such action, citing the Maharaja's extensive independent territory from time immemorial.

This episode illustrates the importance of territorial status and political recognition in Tripura's history during the colonial period.

3. Boundaries of Hill Tipperah in the Imperial Gazetteer of India

The Imperial Gazetteer of India, in its entry on Hill Tipperah State, provides a detailed description of the state's nineteenth-century boundaries:

“Bounded on the north by the Assam District of Sylhet; on the west by the Bengal Districts of Tipperah and Noakhali; on the south by Noakhali and Chittagong; and on the east by the Lushai country and the Chittagong Hill Tracts. The western boundary of the State, where it adjoins the Regulation District of Tipperah, was fixed by arbitration in 1854. The eastern boundary, separating it from the Lushai country and the Chittagong Hill Tracts, is formed, according to the Surveyor-General’s map of 1875, by the Lungai river, between the Haichek and Jampui ranges to its source in Betling Sib peak, the line next runs in an irregular line to the Dolajari peak, and then along the Sardeng range and the Pheni river, till the latter enters Noakhali District”.

This account identifies the neighbouring districts, hill ranges, rivers and peaks used to describe Hill Tipperah's boundaries during the nineteenth century. It records that the western boundary adjoining the British-administered Regulation District of Tipperah was settled through arbitration in 1854. The eastern boundary is described with reference to the Surveyor-General's map of 1875 and a sequence of natural geographical features.

The gazetteer is an important historical source for reconstructing the boundaries of the princely state during the British period. Its description should be understood as a record of that period, rather than as a definitive account of Tripura's boundaries throughout its entire history.

4. Historical Significance

The historical boundaries of Tripura reflect centuries of political change, territorial negotiation and relations with neighbouring powers. Traditional accounts of the kingdom's wider extent, the territorial status of Hill Tipperah under British rule, and the boundary descriptions recorded in colonial gazetteers each contribute to understanding the region's historical geography.

The distinction between Hill Tipperah, British-administered Tipperah District and the royal estate of Chakla Roshnabad is particularly important. Although connected through the history of the Tripura monarchy, these territories had different political and administrative statuses.

Read alongside historical maps, boundary arbitration records, royal documents and other contemporary sources, the Imperial Gazetteer of India provides a valuable foundation for researching the changing territorial geography of Tripura.

Reference

Hunter, W. W. (ed.), The Imperial Gazetteer of India, entry on “Hill Tipperah State.” Consult the verified edition used for this article and include its volume and page number in the final bibliographic record.

Historical Overview

History of the Census — From Ancient Civilizations to the Royal State of Tripura

Census-taking has a long history. From ancient civilizations to modern states, rulers and administrations have gathered information about their populations to understand settlement, resources, taxation, and public responsibilities.

In the modern period, population censuses developed into systematic exercises recording information such as population size, households, occupations, literacy, and other social characteristics. In India, census records became important sources for studying demographic change and the history of administration.

The history of census-taking in Tripura must be understood in its own historical context. Census exercises conducted in British India in years such as 1872, 1881, and 1901 belong to the wider Indian census history; they should not, by themselves, be treated as proof of an official census conducted under Tripura's Royal Administration.

An important record of the Royal Administration is Census Bibarani, associated with the first official census of Tripura under the Royal Administration. The report was published in Bengali, with the year recorded as 1343 in the Tripura Era (Tring). The historical source associates the census with effect from 26 February 1931.

Such records are valuable for studying the population and administrative practices of the former princely state. They provide a historical foundation for examining how the state documented its people and how census information can help researchers understand Tripura's past.

After Tripura's integration into India, census records continued to provide important evidence of population change, settlement patterns, and social development. Read together, the historical records of the Royal Administration and later censuses help trace changes across different periods of Tripura's history.

Rare Historical Record

History of the Census in Tripura — Census Bibarani, an Important Record of the Royal Administration

Census Bibarani is an important historical record associated with the first official census conducted under Tripura's Royal Administration. It offers researchers a window into the state's population documentation and administrative history.

The report is written in Bengali. Its publication year is recorded as 1343 of the Tripura Era (Tring). This calendar designation must not be confused with the Bengali Era (Bangabda), which is a separate calendar system.

According to the historical information available for this record, the census was given effect from 26 February 1931. This date and the era designation should be cited as recorded in the source when the report is discussed or catalogued.

The report is associated with Senior Nayeb Thakur Sumendra Chandra Debbarma. The officer's name and any academic credentials should be checked against the original publication before a definitive catalogue entry is prepared.

As a rare administrative publication, Census Bibarani deserves preservation and careful study. Its original wording, publication details, and historical context are important for future researchers of Tripura.
